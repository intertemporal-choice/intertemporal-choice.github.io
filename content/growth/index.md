# Growth Theory

By conventional measures, the value of goods and services produced per person today is 20–30 times higher in the richest countries than in the poorest countries. Furthermore, income per capita is positively related to alternative measures of the quality of life such as infant mortality, life expectancy, nutrition, health, and literacy; thus, the 20–30 factor arguably understates international differences in standards of living. Output per capita in rich countries today is perhaps 40–50 times higher than 400 years ago, with improvements in qualitative indicators as well.

Why are some countries rich and other countries poor? Or, as the philosopher P. J. O'Rourke put it, "Why do some places thrive and prosper, while others just suck?" If there is any single thing we would most want economics to explain, this is it. The attempt to answer this question is in fact what motivated Adam Smith to write *The Wealth of Nations* {cite:p}`smith:wealth`, which created the discipline of economics. While the branch of economics that deals with these questions is called the theory of economic growth, it is really more about the level than about the growth rate of output.

If pressed to name the single most important factor in explaining sustainable differences in the wealth and poverty of nations, the answer many economists would give is "the rule of law." A country where the rule of law holds is a country where there is a well-defined set of laws which are well understood by the population, which apply to the government and its officials as well as to the population, and which are reasonably well enforced. The exact specifics of the law seem to be less important than whether any kind of law exists. Countries where there is no effective rule of law are places where the strongest simply take what they want from others, whether they hold government posts or are in private life. In such a place it would be foolish to engage in any sort of productive economic enterprise, because as soon as you succeeded in making any profits, someone with more power would come and steal them from you.

While economists have begun to grapple seriously with models of the rule of law and with relating those models to reality, the standard theory of economic growth implicitly assumes that effective rule of law applies in the background. The analysis in this part therefore does not really apply to countries where the rule of law does not exist, that is, to the poorest countries in the world; it is more a theory of how countries grow once they have established the rule of law. A prerequisite for any of it to make sense is the existence of the rule of law.

Many of the field's important themes were first touched on in *The Wealth of Nations*. Economists' attention to the topic since has been decidedly episodic: there was not really a separate field of long-run growth until the 1950s, when the foundations of its modern study were laid by {cite:t}`solow:contribution`. An intense burst of theoretical and empirical work followed, lasting until the late 1960s, when growth theory went into decline. In the mid-1980s, a couple of influential papers by Paul Romer {cite:p}`romer:growth` relaunched the study of growth into the limelight, where it has remained ever since.

The Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans model is the workhorse of this chapter. A representative
household chooses consumption over an infinite horizon, with capital as the only state
variable. Equilibrium traces a saddle path in the consumption-capital plane. The first
several handouts stay inside that framework and add pieces to it: government purchases
and taxation, and a decentralization that lets competitive markets reproduce the
planner's allocation.

We then turn to the model's central embarrassment. Unless productivity growth arrives
from outside, the model predicts no long-run growth in per capita income at all (the
thing it was built to explain enters as an assumption).
The endogenous growth literature answers by finding some factor that households can
accumulate without bound, and the handouts on Romer, Rebelo, and Lucas each pick a
different candidate. A final handout swaps the infinitely lived household for Blanchard's
perpetual youth structure, which restores a finite planning horizon while keeping the
model tractable.
